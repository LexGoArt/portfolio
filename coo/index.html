How I work

Seven rules I apply from week one. They are boring on purpose: an operation that depends on someone being clever every morning is not an operation, it is a rescue.

  1. 01

    Map first, decide second

    Before anything is built I run discovery: interviews, customer journeys, competitor checks. Most of the savings come from work that never starts.

  2. 02

    One source of truth

    A dozen spreadsheets that disagree is not data, it is a negotiation. I collapse them into one live registry everyone reads, including the sales floor.

  3. 03

    Roles, not heroes

    Governance framework, written role and process documentation, reporting standards. The system has to survive the person who built it leaving.

  4. 04

    Priority is calculated, not argued

    MoSCoW and WSJF on the backlog. When the ranking is arithmetic, the loudest voice in the room stops setting the roadmap.

  5. 05

    Risks are named before they bite

    RAID logs kept live, not reconstructed after the fact. A risk written down is a decision waiting; a risk unwritten is a surprise with a date on it.

  6. 06

    AI in the process, not in the deck

    I do not run AI pilots. I take a recurring task, measure what it costs today, rebuild it with AI, and keep it only if the number moves.

  7. 07

    Reporting a board reads in two minutes

    A KPI matrix, not a deck. If an owner needs a meeting to understand the status, the reporting has failed, not the owner.

Where AI actually pays

I do not sell AI. I use it, and I can show the invoice.

Most "AI transformation" stops at a pilot nobody adopts. My approach is narrower and duller: find the tasks that repeat, price them honestly, rebuild them, keep score.

  • Documentation and reporting. The cycle from raw numbers to a document an owner can read, compressed from days to hours.
  • Analytics prep. The unglamorous 80% before an analyst starts thinking, handed to a machine.
  • Internal tools. Registries, calculators and dashboards built in days instead of being queued behind an IT roadmap.
  • What I do not automate. Anything where a wrong answer is expensive and nobody would notice it was wrong.

My first 90 days

The same sequence every time. I do not change anything in month one, because the first thing an operation needs is an honest picture of itself.

Days 1–30

Look, do not touch

  • Map every process, project and reporting line as they actually run
  • Interview the people doing the work, not only the people describing it
  • Find where money and time leak, with numbers attached

You getAn honest map and a ranked list of leaks.

Days 31–60

Fix the loudest leak

  • Close the single most expensive gap, visibly, so the change is credible
  • Stand up one source of truth for whatever people argue about most
  • Install a reporting cadence the owner reads in two minutes

You getOne measurable win and reporting you can trust.

Days 61–90

Make it hold without me

  • Write the governance: roles, processes, delivery standard
  • Automate the repetitive parts and measure what that saved
  • Hand over dashboards and the operating rhythm to the team

You getA system that runs when I am on holiday.
